How many ultrasonic sensors should the hat use?

We compared 7 layouts against your concerns about coverage, delay, GPIO, loose pins, power, voltage dividers and mounting. We ran the real struck-by detector on simulated approaches to measure coverage and warning time.

Build 4 rear sensors and remove the top one. Fewer than 4 only if the bench test earns it.

The top sensor gave no warning for dropped objects in the 5-sensor setup. Removing it makes rear warnings faster, and frees 2 pins and 1 divider. A wider measured beam could cut the rear count to 3 or 2 with the same coverage, so run the 30-minute beam test before the CAD team finalizes the mount.

Your questions, answered

ConcernAnswer
Is the top sensor needed?No. A dropped object falls too fast for it to help. But "the hat protects us" isn't the reason to drop it: large objects can still injure.
Same coverage with fewer than 5?Yes, 4 keeps all the rear coverage. 3 or 2 only match it if your sensors' real beam is about 20° or 30° wide. The datasheet says under 15°.
Does fewer mean less delay?Yes. Each sensor removed takes 30 ms off every sweep. The delay comes from waiting for echoes in turn, not from processing.
Enough GPIO pins?Yes, this isn't a real limit. The 5-sensor design uses 16 of 26 pins. The recommended one uses 14.
Loose pins?The real risk. Fix it with a soldered hub board and no jumper wires on the Pi header.
Enough power?Yes. All the sensors together draw about 75 mA of a roughly 1.9 A worst case, on a 3 A supply.
A divider for every sensor?Yes, one per echo line, but they can all sit on one small board at the Pi rather than on the hat.
Where do they mount?One printed curved pod on the back of the shell, with boards standing vertically. Nothing on the crown.

The top sensor and your statistic

We couldn't verify the 47% back / 23% top figures. We found no source for them. The nearest lead was an old OSHA-hosted document on head injuries, which we couldn't open; a search summary described it as reporting many blows to the back of the head, but we haven't read it. If someone has the original source, please share it. The decision doesn't depend on these numbers, as the table above shows.

The physics decides it. We dropped a 20 cm object straight onto the hat from each height, with a clean echo every time, which is the best case for this sensor:

From higher up, the object is already moving fast when it enters the 2.5 m tracking range, and it's gone in a few sweeps. Small objects like bolts may not echo at all. The top sensor can see slower overhead motion, like a load moving down at a steady 3 m/s. That is the one case where it helps.

Dropping it doesn't remove overhead events from the system. The accelerometer still detects the hit, so the clip and report still happen. Only the advance warning goes, and it wasn't working for dropped objects anyway.

A caution for the pitch: don't say "the hard hat handles anything from above." Hard hats are built mainly for top impacts, but heavy objects can still cause serious injury. Say instead that a sensor can't warn in time for a falling object.

A knock-on effect: the top motor would no longer give direction warnings. Keep it for the fall and impact buzz, or remove it to save another pin.

Delay

Sound takes time, so sensors have to take turns. Each gets a 30 ms slot, and the detector needs several readings per sensor. On a laptop, the detector code took about 3 µs per sample, so even if a Pi is many times slower, it's tiny next to a 120 ms sweep.

SensorsSweepTime to detect, 4 readingsTime to detect, 3 readings
5150 ms750 ms600 ms
4120 ms600 ms480 ms

260 ms300 ms240 ms

Going from 5 sensors with 4 readings to 4 sensors with 3 readings cuts detection from 750 ms to 480 ms. That's a 36% cut with no loss of rear coverage. Both changes are config settings. Using 3 readings added no false alarms in the backward-walk test, but only real recordings can confirm that.

Voltage dividers: can they be combined?

Every echo line needs its own level shift, since joining two echo outputs at one divider connects them together. They don't have to sit on the hat, though.

What happens when something comes loose

FailureResultPrevention
A 5 V jumper slips onto a GPIO pinCan permanently kill the Pi. The worst case.No loose jumpers on the header. Solder to a proto HAT that plugs on.
Divider's ground-side resistor comes offPin sees 5 V through 1 kΩ, about 1.4 mA into its protection diode. Out of spec, and it may survive.Solder dividers on the hub board.
Sensor ground comes off but 5 V stays onSensor current looks for a return path through its signal pins and into the Pi.Use a single 4-pin connector per sensor. Add 1 kΩ in series on each trigger.
Signal wire comes offHarmless electrically, but that sensor goes silently blind.Run argus-cli test range before every session.

Wiring plan: solder wires directly to each sensor's pins, with heat-shrink and a strain-relief tie at the pod. Run one 4-wire cable to each sensor, and plug it into a polarized JST-XH socket on the hub. XH may not latch firmly, so add a dab of hot glue once it's tested.

Sensor count barely changes power: dropping one saves about 1%. Runtime is roughly 2.4 hours at an average 1.3 A (estimated, not measured).

The power risk to test is noise, not capacity. Motors starting up put spikes on the 5 V rail, which could cause false readings on the same rail. Put a 100 µF capacitor on the sensor power at the hub. Then read all sensors while buzzing the motors, and look for false readings. Check vcgencmd get_throttled as well.

For the CAD team

An HC-SR04 board is about 45 × 20 × 15 mm. We assumed a shell radius of about 115 mm at the back, so please measure your hat. Spacing along the shell is radius × angle:

LayoutSpacingBoards flat (45 mm wide)Boards vertical (20 mm wide)
4 rear at 15° apart30 mmCollideFits, 10 mm gaps, about 110 mm total
3 rear at 20° apart40 mmCollide by 5 mmFits, about 100 mm total
2 rear at 30° apart60 mmFitsFits
  • Print one curved pod holding all sensors. The angles are built into the part, since a ±5° aiming error can open gaps. Mount it on the back of the shell with a 5° upward tilt to clear the shoulders.
  • Design the pod so the sensor count can change. Finish the angles only after the beam test, or print a second pod.
  • Nothing on the crown. A rigid bracket there sits in the main strike zone and adds a long cable run.
  • Don't drill the shell. Modifying a hard hat can void its certification, so check the maker's manual, including on adhesives. Use clips or a strap.
  • Keep the Pi and battery below the beams so they don't echo. Check that collars and hoods don't either.
  • Check the head-down posture. Workers often look down, which tips rear beams upward by the same angle. Test whether a person behind is still seen at a 30° nod.

Bench tests that settle this

These take about half a day and should happen before the pod is finalized.

  1. Beam width. With boards vertical as mounted, step a 5 cm pipe sideways at 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 m. Record where detection falls below 80%. About 15° wide: 4 sensors. At least 20°: 3 sensors (D). At least 30°: 2 sensors (G). In simulation, with a 30° beam, G reaches 100% of the zone and scores highest, at 480 of 500.
  2. Real range. Record the farthest reliable distance for a pipe, a board and a person in a hoodie. More range is the biggest gain in warning time.
  3. "No echo" pulse. Put a scope or logic analyzer on echo with nothing in front. The datasheet says about 38 ms, but some clones reportedly hold it for 150–200 ms. That would stop a sensor re-firing on a 120 ms sweep.
  4. Motor noise. Read all sensors while buzzing the motors.
  5. Head-down. Repeat a person approaching from behind at a 30° nod.

What's still unproven

  • All coverage and timing numbers come from simulation. None has been tested on real hardware.
  • Results use the datasheet beam, and real sensors differ, which is why the beam test matters.
  • The false-alarm check covers walking backward only. Real recordings need to cover normal work too.
  • Power figures marked as estimates should be confirmed with a USB power meter.
